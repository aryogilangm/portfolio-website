Suroboyo Bus Route Map

I can’t stop watching the trains on Muhammad Raufan’s Tokyo transit map. They glide along the lines and slide into stations, and every time I open it I stay longer than I planned. So I set myself a challenge: build the same kind of map for Suroboyo Bus, with an AI agent doing the engineering side while I make the design decisions.

I wasn’t the first to draw Surabaya’s routes. Transport for Surabaya, a non-profit, had already made a map, and it influenced mine a lot, layout included. The routes and stop names are still Surabaya’s own.

Getting the Routes Right

I built the map one corridor at a time, since each can take a few hours to get right. For every corridor I collected the stop names and their coordinates in a spreadsheet.

My spreadsheet of stop names and their coordinates, one corridor at a time.

My first attempt was to have the agent place those stops on Google Maps. The result had too many turns, and the stops were slightly off even with exact coordinates.

So I asked for two prototypes to compare. Geographic kept the real road shapes, wiggles included. Eight directions locked every segment to 45° angles, so the routes came out tidy and still easy to recognise. I chose Eight directions.

The routes still needed cleaning up, so I redrew each corridor in Figma, using Transport for Surabaya’s map as a guide. I wasn’t changing the style, only tidying the routes.

This is the map I redrew in Figma, to make it easier to implement and build.

Tools Instead of Prompts

Every change to the map meant another prompt, and every prompt cost me time and tokens. Placing the stops alone took many rounds of coordinates and corrections, so I asked for a tool where I drag a stop to where it belongs and save the position. Polishing the design had the same problem. Route thickness, stop size, and the size and roundness of the buses are things I judge by eye, and describing them in prompts would have been slow and fiddly, so I added them as sliders to the same tool. Now I move a slider, look at the map, and decide.

When a decision is about feel, I’d rather build a tool than write a longer prompt.

Designing for The People who Ride

The Tokyo map has a light and a dark mode, so I started with the same. Then I thought about who this map is for. Buses carry people who get on and off at every stop, each with somewhere to be. So I made four themes that follow their day: early morning, midday, afternoon and night.

Night was easy. Instead of pure black I used a dark navy, like a clear night sky. The other three were hard, and Pinterest gave me nothing. Then one evening I looked at the cover of American Football - American Football (LP3) and it matched the early morning in my head. I went back through my Apple Music library and found two more covers for midday and afternoon.

I gave Claude Code the colours and mood of each cover and asked for ten backgrounds. I picked two or three, asked for ten more based on those, and repeated. The first rounds still looked like AI to me, so it took several passes.

Focussing on Two Corridors

The Tokyo map lets you focus on one line and fades the rest. I wanted the same, but one line wasn’t enough. Many Suroboyo Bus stops are shared by two or three corridors, so riders often change corridors mid-trip, and with one corridor in focus you can’t see where the trip continues.

I asked friends who ride Suroboyo Bus regularly how they travel. From what they’ve seen and their own habits, they usually change corridors once, at a transit stop, to reach where they’re going.

So the map lets you focus on two corridors, the one you board and the one you change to.

Outcome

I built this to learn. It was a case study, and a way to see how far I can go working with an AI agent. But I liked the result more than I expected. I’ve caught myself opening it to check a route, and I can see it helping other people understand Suroboyo Bus more easily. I haven’t decided whether to release it. If I do, it will be free.
